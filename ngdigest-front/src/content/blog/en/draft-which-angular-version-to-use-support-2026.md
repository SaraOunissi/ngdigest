The question never comes from a developer. It comes from an application owner, at the end of a meeting, in a tone that promises nothing good: "we're on Angular 20 — are we still supported?". And the honest answer — "until November 28, 2026" — is valuable precisely because it is verifiable, dated, and leads straight to a quote. Since the summer of 2026, the calendar behind that answer has changed pace: Angular now ships **one major every twelve months**, with **24 months of support** per version instead of 18.

## What changed, and what didn't

Three lines of the official docs moved, and only one of them is structural.

1. **The major cadence goes from 6 to 12 months.** The docs now state "a major release every 12 months", with an explicit note: *until v22, Angular had a 6-month major release cycle*. v22 is the hinge, and **v23 is targeted for June 2027** — so there will be **no** major in the autumn of 2026, contrary to what ten years of habit suggest.
2. **Minors go from 1-3 to 4-6 per major.** That's the counterweight: feature delivery doesn't slow down, it moves into the minors, roughly one every eight weeks.
3. **Total support goes from 18 to 24 months**: **12 months active** (regular updates and patches) then **12 months LTS** (critical fixes and security patches only). The old split was 6 + 12.

> What is slowing down isn't Angular. It's the windows during which anyone is allowed to break your code.

The rest of the contract is unchanged: breaking changes stay confined to majors, minors stay backward-compatible, patches keep shipping almost weekly. In other words, nothing was taken away — the period during which a codebase can be left alone just got longer.

## The three support windows, dated

As of September 12, 2026, only three versions are covered. These dates come from the official table of supported versions.

| Version | Status | Active support until | LTS until |
|---|---|---|---|
| **22** (released 2026-06-03) | Active | June 2027 | June 2028 |
| **21** (released 2025-11-19) | LTS | ended 2026-06-03 | June 2027 |
| **20** (released 2025-05-28) | LTS | ended 2025-11-19 | **2026-11-28** |

Everything older — **v2 through v19** — is no longer supported at all. If the client is on v19 or below, the conversation is no longer "when do we migrate" but "how long have we been exposed".

:::keyfigures The new contract, in three numbers
- neutral | 12 months | between two majors (down from 6)
- good | 24 months | of support per version (12 active + 12 LTS, up from 18)
- bad | 2026-11-28 | end of v20 LTS — the only short deadline on the table

:::warn "Supported" doesn't mean "maintained"
A version in LTS receives **only** critical fixes and security patches. v21 today, v20 since November 2025: they are covered, but no longer improved. That's exactly the nuance an application owner misses when told "it's still supported", and it's the one that justifies planning instead of waiting for the deadline.
:::

## "Major" doesn't mean "big feature"

This is the confusion that costs the most in meetings, and it predates the cadence change. SemVer doesn't describe how important a release is, it describes **the risk of breakage**. A major number going up means "something may have broken", not "there are new features". Symmetrically, a minor can carry a substantial feature as long as nothing breaks.

Concretely, on the current cycle: features will land in **22.1, 22.2, 22.3, 22.4, 22.5**, and there will be nothing to do beyond `npm update`. The next time anyone needs to budget time, testing and review is **June 2027**.

That's also what makes the new rhythm legible to an IT department: one annual appointment, known in advance, instead of two half-year windows that get postponed until one is missed.

## Deprecation moves to "one major" — and loses nothing

One line of this change is going to be misread, so it's worth defusing immediately. The deprecation policy went from "at least **two** majors" to "at least **one** major". Read quickly, that looks like the grace period was cut in half.

It wasn't. Two majors at 6 months was about **12 months**. One major at 12 months is… about **12 months** — and the docs say it plainly: the deprecation period lasts *at least 12 months*. The number changed because the unit changed. The real time available to remove a deprecated API is identical.

That kind of detail looks like trivia, right up until someone uses it as an argument to refuse a version bump.

## What it changes for your monitoring and your engagements

For a freelancer, this calendar isn't a technical detail, it's a planning tool. Three practical consequences.

1. **One migration a year, planned rather than endured.** The natural window is the quarter following a major: the teething bugs are out, the `ng update` schematics have stabilised, and third-party libraries have caught up. For v23, that puts the good window in **autumn 2027**.
2. **Never skip a major.** The `ng update` rule hasn't changed: you upgrade **one major at a time**, and the version you upgrade *from* must still be supported. An abandoned v19 is paid for in chained migrations, each with its own breakages.
3. **Track the minors, not the majors.** That's where the next twelve months of features will ship. A "what's new in Angular 23" piece in June 2027 arrives after the battle; the interesting material is in the 22.x line.

Reading the ecosystem by windows is the same skill as reading the market: see [the Angular frontend and fullstack market in 2026](/en/blog/angular-frontend-fullstack-market-2026) and, for the rate side, [an Angular developer's day rate in 2026](/en/blog/angular-developer-daily-rate-2026).

## How to sell it to a client

The sentence that works isn't "you need to migrate". It's **"here's your date, here's what happens after it, here's the work package"**. Three elements, in that order.

First the date, sourced: *"your v20 has only been getting security fixes since November 2025, and that coverage stops on November 28, 2026 — that's the official table of supported versions talking"*. Then the risk, without drama: past that date, no fixes at all, security included, and a dependency that breaks becomes an internal problem. Finally the package: a version upgrade **quoted separately** from everything else, with a written scope — dependency inventory, `ng update` major by major, test pass, migration note.

The new rhythm is itself a commercial argument: **one predictable annual appointment** is easier to budget than two half-year windows arbitrated under pressure. And if the broader freelance positioning question is what's on your mind, the [Angular career guide](/en/career/guide) covers that side.

## Takeaway

One major a year, 24 months of support, v23 in June 2027, and a single short deadline on the table: **November 28, 2026**, the end of v20. The remaining work isn't technical — it's turning a public date into a line on a quote before the client finds the date on their own.
