> *"If you'd been fullstack, we'd have kept you on the project for six months, waiting for front-end work to come back. As it is, we have to let you go."*

I heard that sentence on my last mission. I nearly broke down. November 2025, a consulting firm (ESN) in the Lyon area. Three years with the same client (RTE), a team I clicked with, an Angular front-end mission with a UX/UI hat I'd deliberately chosen. Then one morning I'm told there's no front-end work in the pipeline for at least six months. There is a way to stay — but it's called Java. Spring Boot. Starting tomorrow if you want.

I said no. And I landed on the bench at my ESN right after. It's a decision I'm proud of — and one I'd never have imagined having to make two years ago.

## Turning down Java, and the regret that comes with it

From day one with this client I'd been clear: **I can't stand Java.** Not a pose, not an attitude — a real block, tied to my path and to the identity I've built as a dev. When they offered "you'll do some back-end Java as a transition," I had zero inner room to manoeuvre. I couldn't pretend to say "ok, a few months, we'll see."

And yet — here's the uncomfortable part — I also carry a small regret. Over those three years, I could probably have taken on a few back-end tickets, mentored by a patient senior colleague. I feel like I missed a chance to widen my professional surface while I was safe.

But — and it's the "but" that brings back the clarity — on this particular mission I'd positioned myself **front + UX/UI on purpose, so I'd stop doing fullstack.** I'd just come out of two years of fullstack on my previous mission, and it had worn me down. The market caught up with me without warning. And the worst part: **I'm not an isolated case.** That's what I want to unpack here. With data.

## Has the pure front-end market really tipped over?

Short answer: **yes, and the numbers say so.** I compiled the volume of openings on the main French platforms in May 2026. Here's what I saw:

| Search | Openings (FR, May 2026) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Java Fullstack Spring Boot Angular" | **~1,000** openings | LinkedIn FR |
| "Spring Boot Angular" | **+500** openings | Indeed FR (Apr. 2026) |
| "Java Angular" | **+700** openings | Indeed FR (Feb. 2026) |
| "Front-End Developer" (all stacks) | **~1,000** openings | Indeed FR (May 2026) |

Read that last line carefully. **The total of "pure front-end" openings across every technology** (Angular, React, Vue, Svelte…) is in the same ballpark as **the Java/Angular fullstack combo on its own.** For an Angular dev who wants to stay pure, that means most of the market mentioning "Angular" also demands Java — and the remaining pool is shared with every other front-end dev.

On the ground, I also see it in the shape of the job ads:

- Ads that open with "Angular" and close with "Angular + Spring Boot," sometimes buried at the bottom.
- Descriptions framed as **"front 80% / back 20%"** — a hybrid wording that reassures before nudging you toward the back-end.
- **Transition clauses** in ESN contracts, a switch planned if the pipeline dries up.
- **Mid-mission reconversions** (my case).

And what I see in the Lyon area is even sharper. My ESN branch manager put it plainly: *"In our region it's really the Angular/Java combo that's pushed right now. More than Angular/Node or Angular/.NET."*

On the macro side, the context is well known but worth restating. Here are the three figures that frame the period:

:::keyfigures The French tech market, in three figures
- bad | −18% | IT hiring in 2024 (APEC)
- bad | −19% | Junior profiles specifically (APEC)
- good | +4.3% | Digital growth expected in 2026 — but aimed at cyber / cloud / data (Numeum)
:::

The verdict: the pure Angular front-end job isn't dead, but the market is asking it to mutate. And the mutation ESN recruiters push hardest is toward Java.

## Why this shift?

I don't claim to have the definitive answer. I have hypotheses, some sturdier than others.

### The market is tight, and AI muddies everything

Less tech budget, fewer hires, so each head has to cover more ground. The "T-shape" storytelling has become an "I-shape with three legs." On top of that, my first guess: **specialized devs now sell themselves as fullstack because they know AI will paper over their real lack of expertise on the other side.**

### The "AI does the front-end" myth

To a non-technical manager, AI churns out HTML/CSS/Angular fast, so "front-end is easy now." It's a dangerous shortcut: front-end complexity has moved to architecture, performance, accessibility, distributed state, deferred rendering. **None of that is solved with a prompt.**

## AI isn't a bridge, it's a crutch for your gaps

When I open `claude` on one of my side projects, **I lean on it most for the back-end**: FastAPI, Node, Mongo. Why? Because I know I'm weak there. AI fills my gaps.

On the front-end, though — where I'm at home — **I stay hyper-vigilant.** Even when I let AI generate a component, I re-read it, I watch it, I check Change Detection, accessibility, the signals architecture. A back-end dev probably does the opposite. **AI is a crutch for your gaps — not a replacement for expertise.**

And let's be honest: that argument isn't entirely wrong. On my current mission they really expect fullstack; I stay positioned as front-end, so nobody hassles me too much — but the tickets aren't compartmentalized at all, and I find myself touching Java, which I normally understand nothing about. If I manage, it's **thanks to AI.** So yes, it *can* compensate — at least enough to "do a bit." Except that "doing a bit" isn't "being an expert" — and that's exactly where the market gets it wrong.

> The market buys "fullstack" thinking it's buying double expertise. It's buying single expertise + an AI crutch.

And there's a blind spot: what we lose by pushing front-end devs toward fullstack isn't "just front-end." A pure front-end profile is often a real UX/UI culture, an accessibility skill, an integrator's eye for the responsive and for a design system's consistency. All that baggage, a back-end dev shoved onto the front-end with prompts simply doesn't have.

## What you can do if you refuse to do back-end Java

Good news: you have more options than your ESN lets on. Bad news: none of them is easy.

### Hunt for the niches where pure front-end is still real expertise

Four market pockets still hold up very well:

1. **Internal Angular design systems & libraries.** Big companies are finally investing in shared tooling, which demands real front-end architecture expertise.
2. **Performance & Core Web Vitals audits.** E-commerce budget, direct SEO and conversion impact.
3. **WCAG accessibility.** The European directive is pushing compliance by 2026-2027, and the role is professionalizing.
4. **AngularJS → Angular 21 migrations.** Still plenty of codebases in production, and the know-how is increasingly rare.

:::tip The reflex that changes everything
Look for companies that don't believe in fullstack on principle. One lead told me: "To me fullstack is bullshit, you're never an expert at anything." These companies are rare, but they're gold for a senior pure front-end dev.
:::

### Extend toward Node/Nest rather than Java

On paper it's the natural extension: **TypeScript end to end**, maximum cognitive consistency. But watch the market signal.

:::warn A signal to watch
Node demand is **falling** right now. On Lyon-area listings the Angular/Java combo dominates, Angular/Node slips to second place. A passing move or a deep trend? Worth watching over the next 12 months before betting on it.
:::

### Launch your own product

The moment has never been riper: AI cuts prototyping time by 3–5×, hosting is dirt cheap. It's not an escape hatch — it's the path I'm taking with **NgDigest**. A side product builds you technical, narrative and network capital that makes you less dependent on the job market.

### Leave freelance/ESN for an in-house front-end tech-lead role

A counter-intuitive option. But **the companies that genuinely need a senior pure front-end dev know it**, and they'd rather have them in-house for stability: less imposed fullstack pivoting, more leverage on architecture, a real specialist career.

## Not dead, mutating

The "pure Angular front-end dev" job isn't dead. It's changing face. The centre seats go to fullstack generalists; the edge seats — performance, a11y, design systems, side products — stay with specialists, but they're less lit.

The trap, to avoid at all costs, is letting yourself be pushed into the fullstack mould without loving the back-end. **That's how you build burnouts.** My bet for the next 18 months: own the specialization, choose my missions, invest in my side projects — and accept spells on the bench if that's the price for not betraying myself.
